WASHINGTON (TNND) — Despite a growing list of factors that should bring relief to record home prices, values have remained remarkably sturdy as sellers largely refuse to budge on asking prices and a shrinking share of buyers can afford to be in the market.

Several consecutive years of slow sales have shifted the market decisively in favor of buyers, but has done little to make homeownership more affordable. Higher mortgage rates are pushing up payments while asking prices have stayed high, leaving sellers competing for a shrinking pool of buyers who can afford to be in the market at all.

With so few buyers able to afford to be in the market, the balance of the market has shifted significantly away from sellers as homes sit unsold. The number of buyers in the market hit the lowest level since at least 2013 and there are nearly 63% more sellers than buyers, according to Redfin. It was the fourth month in a row the gap broke a record.

But the growing imbalance has not translated into declines in prices to meaningfully improve the affordability outlook. The median home price for an existing home climbed to $417,500 in September, the 39th consecutive month of annual price increases, according to the National Association of Realtors. New construction also remains out of reach for many with median sales prices of $393,700, according to the National Association of Home Builders.

The gulf between the number of buyers and sellers has made the market overwhelmingly favor buyers, who can be picky and have room to negotiate in most parts of the country. But that is only for people who can afford to be in the market at all, which is getting harder as the costs of homeownership have continued to climb.

“Buyers have tremendous negotiating power in most of the country because so many other would-be buyers have been priced out of the market, or scared off by economic uncertainty,” said Asad Khan, a senior economist at Redfin. “The irony is that in terms of supply, these are some of the best conditions for buyers in many years—but they have arrived at a time when costs are so high that fewer people can afford to take advantage of them.”

Sellers are showing some signs of giving ground on asking prices. In September, the share of active listings that had a price reduction hit 21%, the highest rate for the month since 2018, according to Realtor.com. But sellers are not making the kind of cuts needed to make the housing market affordable, and an increasing share of them are pulling their homes off the market altogether.

Prices have held firm in part because many homeowners are under little pressure to sell. Years of climbing home values have left many with significant equity, while mortgages secured during the pandemic have created an incentive to hold out for higher offers rather than accepting a big discount.

One of the biggest obstacles keeping the market frozen is mortgage rates, which climbed to 7.4% this month, the highest level since November of 2023. Many homeowners have pandemic-era rates of 3% or lower and have been hesitant to give them up to buy a new home at today’s rates even with the cushion of equity.

Mortgage rates have steadily climbed upward since the start of the war with Iran and appear unlikely to fall in the near future. The yield on 10-year Treasurys, which mortgage rates tend to track, has soared to levels not seen since the financial crisis amid concerns about stubborn inflation and mounting levels of government debt.

The cost to buy a home has skyrocketed since the pandemic brought ultra-low rates that helped flood the market with buyers and created intense competition for a limited set of homes. But a return to historical norms in mortgage rates that has sent many buyers to the sidelines has done little to reverse that trend, leaving many would-be buyers priced out.

That squeeze has continued to push the cost of homeownership upward, even as more homes sit on the market. Buyers’ monthly mortgage payments are climbing nearly twice the pace of rent. The typical new home buyer’s monthly payment has climbed 5% compared to last year to $3,037, according to Zillow.

While today’s rates are more in line with historical norms, they are a significant jump from the COVID-era market and are also hitting at the same time as prices hit new records.

Higher rates have also been a hindrance for America’s homebuilders to increase the supply of new homes hitting the market. The U.S. is millions of homes short of what is needed to meet demand, but challenging financial conditions and an uncertain pool of potential buyers are keeping builders from ramping up the pace of new projects.

Construction of new single-family homes is down nearly 5% in 2026 as builders face higher rates, rising construction costs, labor shortages and an iffy housing market that is forcing them to offer discounts and other incentives to move inventory. A sustained slowdown in construction could prolong the affordability crisis when demand recovers and renews competition for too few homes.
